Challenge Room 2

The Reading Room

Six articles. Five real buttons.
One impostor.

What this room simulates

Using a screen reader. Blind and many partially sighted students use screen readers, which read the page aloud — including the hidden labels and structure that sighted users never see.

In this room, one of the six “Read” controls looks like a button but has been built on purpose so that a screen reader doesn’t recognize it as one.

Hint: All six "Read" controls look identical. But only five are real buttons — the sixth is a styled <div> pretending to be one. The keyboard can reach all of them, however one will be read out differently. Enable VoiceOver (Cmd+F5 on Mac) or NVDA on Windows, navigate by buttons (B in browse mode), and notice that one article button is read out differently.
I can’t work this out — show me how

How to solve it

  1. Turn on a screen reader: VoiceOver on a Mac (Cmd+F5), or NVDA (free) or Narrator (Ctrl+Win+Enter) on Windows.
  2. Jump between the buttons on the page. In NVDA, press B. In VoiceOver, open the Rotor (Control+Option+U) and use the arrow keys to choose Form Controls.
  3. You’ll hear five “Read the article… button” announcements. The sixth article, Built for Everyone, never comes up — its control isn’t a real button.
  4. That’s the impostor. Turn off the screen reader and click the Read control under Built for Everyone with your mouse.

No screen reader? Try reaching that last “Read” control with Tab and pressing Enter — nothing happens. Then click it with your mouse.

Why this matters for students

To a sighted mouse user, all six controls are identical. To a student using a screen reader, the sixth one barely exists — it isn’t announced as a button and can’t be pressed from the keyboard. They’d never open that article, and might not even know they’d missed it. This happens whenever something is built to look like a button without actually being one.

You Found the Impostor

That last "Read" control looked exactly like the other five — same style, same label. But it was a <div>, not a <button>. A mouse user can't tell the difference, but to someone who cannot rely on their vision, this labeling makes all the difference in being able to interact with the page or not. Identical on screen, completely different underneath. That's why semantic HTML matters.
